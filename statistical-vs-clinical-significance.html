Statistical vs. clinical significance: why p < .05 is not the end of the story

November 2026 · 6 min read · Melika Mirzabeygi

A statistically significant result tells you that an observed difference is unlikely to be explained by chance alone, given the assumptions of the test. It does not tell you how large the difference is, or whether it would matter to the person receiving the treatment. With a large enough sample, even a trivial difference can produce a very small p-value.

This gap matters most in mental-health research, where outcomes are often rating scales and the question patients and clinicians actually ask is: was the change big enough to feel?

Three tools that answer a different question

Effect sizes. An effect size describes the magnitude of a difference or association on a scale that does not depend on sample size. Reporting it with a confidence interval shows both how big the effect probably is and how precisely it was estimated. Sullivan and Feinn make the case that the effect size, not the p-value, is the main finding of a study [3].

The minimal clinically important difference (MCID). Jaeschke, Singer and Guyatt introduced the MCID as the smallest change in a score that patients perceive as beneficial. They estimated it by anchoring changes in questionnaire scores to patients' own global ratings of change [1]. Defining an MCID before a trial lets you ask whether the observed difference crosses a threshold that matters, not just whether it differs from zero.

The reliable change index (RCI). Working in psychotherapy research, Jacobson and Truax proposed judging change for each individual: first, whether the change exceeds what measurement error alone could produce (the reliable change index); second, whether the person ends up in the range of a functional rather than a dysfunctional population [2]. Together these give a two-part criterion for clinically significant change.

In short: the p-value asks “is there an effect?”, the effect size asks “how big?”, and the MCID and RCI ask “big enough to matter, and for whom?”

Practical reporting checklist

  • Report an effect size with a 95% confidence interval for every primary outcome.
  • Where a validated MCID exists for your measure, name it in the protocol and use it in the sample-size calculation.
  • Report the proportion of participants who reached a clinically meaningful or reliable change, not only group means.
  • Interpret non-significant results with their confidence intervals: “no evidence of a difference” is not “evidence of no difference”.
  • State in the Discussion whether the observed effect is likely to matter in practice, and why.

Why this matters for your manuscript

Reviewers increasingly ask authors to interpret effects, not just test them. Planning for clinical significance at the design stage also makes sample-size justifications more convincing, because the target difference is tied to something patients care about.
